<h2 id="the-first-seed">The first seed</h2>

In 2017, I began gathering titles and bibliographic information for American Haredi children's books in English, in preparation for my dissertation, [Reading the World: American Haredi Children's Literature, 1980-2000](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_etds/4299/). I quickly discovered that writing about this corpus of texts posed a very specific problem: the lack of a central list for all titles. I began trawling WorldCat, Harvard's online catalog, and Hebrew Union College's online catalog for titles and information, only to realize that many catalog records lacked information or included incorrect information. I continued to gather titles and information, knowing that a survey of the corpus couldn't be done well without a complete list, but still having to write my dissertation. During this process, I spoke to librarians in the [CUNY Graduate Center](https://www.gc.cuny.edu/) (where I was studying) for guidance on best practices in recording bibliographic information. This was perhaps the very first seed of the project as it exists today.

In April 2021, I defended my dissertation and earned my PhD. I continued to teach at CUNY colleges, and I continued to gather bibliographic information and write about Haredi children's literature. A large and unwieldy spreadsheet grew from 2017 to 2022, aided by friends and acquaintances on social media remembering the books of their youth, digging through their own or their parents' basements, and lending me physical copies so I could verify information.

<h2 id="following-the-books">Following the books</h2>

I moved to Pittsburgh for a job as Visiting Lecturer in Literature at the University of Pittsburgh in 2022. [Pitt's Jewish Studies program](https://www.jewishstudies.pitt.edu/) awarded me two grants when I was there, one for book purchases and one for research travel. In May 2023, I traveled to Boston, MA, where I spent two weeks sitting in the [Widener Reading Room](https://library.harvard.edu/libraries/widener) at Harvard, paging through hundreds of books which had been prepared for me in advance by Vardit Samuels and Elizabeth Vernon, the Judaica librarians. I filled in missing bibliographic information for titles already in my spreadsheet, added heaps of new titles I had not yet found, and developed a few new techniques for capturing useful data.

In the following year, during a postdoctoral fellowship at the [University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign's iSchool](https://ischool.illinois.edu/), I continued in the same vein as before, collecting bibliographic material, presenting conference papers, and writing journal articles and book chapter drafts. I also worked with the UIUC librarian for Digital Humanities, discussing strategies and factors to consider for building a digital database, as I had been calling it then.

<h2 id="from-academia-to-librarianship">From academia to librarianship</h2>

Bringing me even closer to the project, in September 2024 I shifted from academia to librarianship and took up a position as [Hebraica Library Specialist](https://www.library.upenn.edu/staff/dainy-bernstein) at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://www.library.upenn.edu/vanpelt). The role is not related to Haredi literature or children's literature, but what I hadn't realized when accepting the job offer was that it would give me access to the tools I needed to really get this project going.

Access to OCLC, the backend of WorldCat where cataloging work takes place, allowed me to see much more information about each title. Since cataloging is part of my job, I also learned how to read a MARC (Machine Readable Catalog) record for information that doesn't always appear on the user end—[indicators and delimiters for each field](https://folgerpedia.folger.edu/Interpreting_MARC_records) which encode important metadata about the data.

That access alone probably would not have propelled the project as far as it has come in the past couple of years. What did get me there was the [Association of Jewish Libraries](https://jewishlibraries.org/)' certificate program for Judaica Librarianship, in partnership with the [Jewish Theological Seminary.](https://www.jtsa.edu/) After taking courses covering a wide variety of topics relevant to Judaica librarianship, each participant completed a 100-hour internship. When I proposed being supervised by a Digital Humanities librarian and working on my "database," the certificate committee eagerly approved my plan, and I got to work.

We did discuss the term "database," though. A true database includes the items themselves, not just their bibliographic data, and I had no intention of violating copyright and putting full texts online. Since I planned to include information about libraries holding each title to enable researchers to find the books more quickly than I had, we considered the term "union catalog." Ultimately, when it became clear to me that including location information would be more difficult than I had anticipated, I decided to call it what it is: a bibliography or annotated bibliography.

<h2 id="bringing-it-to-life">Bringing the bibliography to life</h2>

I worked with two DH librarians at Penn, [Matt Hunter](https://www.library.upenn.edu/author/matthew-hunter) and [Cynthia Heider](https://www.library.upenn.edu/staff/cynthia-heider), to deepen my understanding of and skill in DH methods, especially as they applied to my project. I did a lot of coding and played around with a lot of fun tools. I ran searches in OCLC for publishers, authors, and titles. I gathered all the ISBNs I had and ran those through OCLC as well. When I was satisfied with the list of records in my Save File, I exported all the records and ran them through [MARCEdit](https://marcedit.reeset.net/) to convert them into csv format and began cleaning up the data in [OpenRefine](https://openrefine.org/).

As I had seen when gathering data from WorldCat's user side, I quickly realized just how messy the data from OCLC was. Cataloging practices and guidelines have changed many times over the years, so a record created in the 1970s, when catalogs first began to go digital, may have very different methods of recording the same information as a record created in 2020. In addition, the idiosyncrasies of the Haredi publishing industry are not always apparent to the librarian cataloging each title, so information about title, subtitle, author, editor, edition, and more appears differently across multiple records.

I spent many happy hours with my data and completed the 100 hours for the certificate's internship. Afterwards, I spoke to [Laura Newman Eckstein](https://www.library.upenn.edu/staff/laura-newman-eckstein), a curator of Digital Humanities at Penn who specializes in Jewish Studies. She heard what I wanted the project to be, what I had done so far, what obstacles and frustrations had come up, and said, "I could build that really easily."

The current iteration of Kinder Sefarim is a combination of my work over the past decade and Laura's digital expertise, with generous helpings of all the people and institutions who supported my work throughout the years.
